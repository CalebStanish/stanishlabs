5 Things Your Small Business Website Actually Needs (and What You Can Skip)

Most small-business owners get oversold on their website. Someone offers you animations, a chatbot, "AI-powered" features—and skips the handful of things that actually make a website work for a contractor or local shop. Here's what actually matters, and what you can safely leave off.

1. A homepage that says what you do and where, immediately

Above the fold—before anyone has to scroll—a visitor should know what you do, who you serve, and where you're located. That last part matters more than owners often think: naming your actual service area (not just "serving the tri-state area") is what lets both people and search engines match you to local searches. A big generic slideshow that buries this information costs you visitors in the first five seconds.

2. A contact method that doesn't quietly lose leads

Test your own contact form the way a stranger would. A surprising number of small-business sites have a contact form that's silently broken, or one that just opens the visitor's email app—which fails outright on plenty of phones and browsers. If you can't remember the last time you personally submitted your own form to check it works, that's worth doing today.

3. Pages Google can actually read and rank

You don't need to "do SEO" as some ongoing mystery service. You need the fundamentals done correctly, once: a real page title and description on every page, a sitemap, mobile-friendly layout, and reasonably fast load times. Most of the ranking gap between small local businesses and their competitors comes down to these basics being missing entirely, not some advanced tactic.

4. Photos of your actual work

Real job-site photos—even ones taken on a phone—build more trust than polished stock photography of a generic handshake or hard hat. People hiring a local contractor are specifically trying to avoid a stranger; a site that looks like every other stock template works against that.

5. A plan for who keeps it current

Hours, pricing, services, and seasonal offers drift out of date fast. Decide up front—even if the answer is "I'll do it myself twice a year"—who's responsible for updates. A site that's visibly stale (an expired promotion still on the homepage, hours that are wrong on a holiday) undermines the trust it's supposed to build.

What you can skip, at least at first

  • Custom animations and parallax effects. They rarely change whether someone contacts you, and they slow down the page on the phone most of your visitors are using.
  • A blog you won't maintain. A blog with one post from three years ago looks worse than no blog. Only start one if you'll actually keep it up.
  • Live chat widgets before you have the traffic to staff them. An unanswered chat box is worse than no chat box.
  • Full e-commerce features for a service business. If you're not selling physical inventory online, a shopping cart is complexity you don't need.

None of this requires a big budget or a big agency—it requires getting the basics right and not paying for the parts that don't move the needle. If you'd rather talk through what your specific business actually needs, that's exactly the kind of conversation I have for free before any work starts.
